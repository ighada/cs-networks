01

What you have to deliver

Each group of three picks one concept from the course and builds a motion demo that explains itself. The test is simple: if I open your link while none of you are in the room, do I understand it? If yes, you have the marks.

✓ Required

  • One working demo — a link or a file that opens and runs on its own: an interactive page, or a video/GIF that loops.
  • An on-screen title phrased as a question, shown before any motion: "What happens to a signal between the two ends?"
  • A caption line that changes with the motion, saying what is happening right now. This replaces the presenter's voice.
  • Labels on every moving element — layer names, header fields, numbers (seq, TTL, ms, Mbps).
  • Something that changes — a button, a slider, or a before/after pair, with a visible hint that the viewer may try it.
  • A contributions panel inside the demo: each student's name and exactly what she did ("Noura — built the queue animation and the delay maths"). Three lines, no more.
  • References written inside the demo itself: chapter and slide number, plus any outside source.
  • It loops, or has a replay button — the viewer can run it again without help.

✗ Not accepted

  • A PowerPoint talk or slides to be read out. There is no presentation slot and no presenter.
  • A demo that needs explaining — if someone has to stand beside it and talk, it is not finished.
  • A wall of text in the corner of the screen standing in for the narration. One changing line, not a paragraph.
  • Static figures from the book or the course slides with no motion of your own.
  • A ready-made YouTube clip — fine as a reference, not as the deliverable.
  • Unlabelled animation — boxes sliding around with no idea what they are.
  • Several concepts in one demo (TCP + DNS + routing together).

Six rules of a self-explaining demo

01 · One idea

The animation answers one question. For example: "why does store-and-forward add delay?" — nothing more.

02 · Show state, not arrows

An arrow says "it went". A growing queue, a filling table, a sliding window say why.

03 · Put numbers on it

A delay counter in ms, seq/ack numbers, a TTL counting down, a loss rate. Numbers turn a drawing into a measurement.

04 · Give the audience a knob

One slider (noise, send rate, buffer size) creates the strongest moment of the talk: "watch what happens when I turn this up."

05 · Break it on purpose

The happy path is interesting once. Loss, collision, a full queue, a flipped bit — that is where the understanding happens.

06 · The silent test

Hand the demo to someone who has not taken the course and say nothing. If she tells you what she saw, you are done. If she asks "what is this?", go back and fix it.

Making it unforgettable — not just understood

The point of this assignment is not that your classmate understands while watching — it is that she still remembers after the exam, and after a year. Understanding fades; a mental picture stays. Five things that work:

  • One strong image. The whole concept collapses into a single scene your classmate could redraw from memory. IP addresses staying fixed while MAC addresses are swapped at every hop — that is a scene. Six scattered diagrams are not.
  • Break it. Memory holds onto failure far better than success. Let the queue overflow, the bit flip, the segment vanish. The happy path is forgotten; the moment it fell apart is not.
  • Put both states side by side. Before and after on one screen, not one after the other. The brain stores the difference, not the state.
  • A number that jumps. A counter doubling or snapping to zero in front of them. A moving number sticks harder than any sentence.
  • Name it in a short phrase. Put one line on screen that sums the concept up: "the bottleneck decides". That phrase is what comes back to her in the exam.

05

Tools you can build this with

No programming required. The tools are ordered easiest first — and the easiest one earns full marks if the motion is clear.

PPT

PowerPoint — Morph + Animation Pane easiest

Fastest route to a respectable result: duplicate the slide, nudge the box, set the transition to Morph — PowerPoint animates between them. Use Appear/Wipe for headers arriving and a Motion Path for a packet travelling. Then export the deck straight to MP4 — the video file is the deliverable, not a talk.

PT

Cisco Packet Tracer — Simulation mode easy

The strongest ready-made option: build a small topology, switch to Simulation Mode and run a ping — Packet Tracer animates PDU envelopes between devices and opens up each layer's contents. Screen-record the motion. Excellent for ARP, DHCP, switch learning and routing.

CV

Canva / Figma easy

Canva has ready animations and one-click GIF/MP4 export. Figma's Smart Animate tweens between frames — the same idea as Morph with tighter design control.

WS

Wireshark + ping / traceroute easy

Not an animation tool, but it gives you real data to animate: capture an actual three-way handshake and turn the real seq/ack numbers into your animation. The pairing of a real capture with an animation built on it earns the creativity point outright.

GIF

ScreenToGif / LICEcap / OBS Studio easy

For recording any on-screen motion as a GIF or MP4 that loops on its own — useful when your demo lives in a tool that cannot be shared as a link.

</>

HTML + CSS + JavaScript (canvas) medium

Its advantage is interactive sliders the viewer can try alone, which is exactly what earns the "moment that sticks" mark. Start with plain CSS transitions, like the example above — even with no canvas at all the result is strong.

p5

p5.js medium

An online editor (editor.p5js.org) built for animated drawing in a few lines. Great for signal transformation, waveforms and collisions.

Mn

Manim (Python) advanced

The library behind 3Blue1Brown's videos. Cinematic output, but it needs Python and time. Pick it only if one of you is comfortable with Python.

GNS

GNS3 / EVE-NG advanced

Real emulators running actual system images. Very accurate, but they have no built-in animation like Packet Tracer — you would screen-record and overlay your own visuals.

06

Before you submit

You hand in one link or one file. There is no presentation slot, no slides, and nobody standing up to explain.

Checklist

  • The silent test: someone outside the course opened it while you said nothing, and could tell you what she saw.
  • One concept, written on screen as a question before the motion starts.
  • A caption line that changes with the steps and says what is happening now.
  • Every moving element carries a label (layer, field or number).
  • It loops or has an obvious replay button.
  • The contributions panel is inside the demo: three lines, each student and what she did.
  • References inside the demo: chapter and slide number, plus any outside source.
  • The link was tested on another machine and a phone, and runs with nothing to install.

Anatomy of a self-explaining demo

  • 1 · The question: one line at the top, always visible. "Why does the queue grow when arrivals speed up?"
  • 2 · The stage: the drawing and the motion, with everything in it named.
  • 3 · The caption line: under the stage, changing with each step. This is your voice, written down.
  • 4 · The counters: moving numbers (sent, dropped, ms) that back up what the caption claims.
  • 5 · The knob: a slider or button, with a "try me" hint beside it.
  • 6 · The footer: contributions panel plus references.
